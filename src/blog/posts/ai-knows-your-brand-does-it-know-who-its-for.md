Ask an AI assistant what your company does, and it might give you a perfectly reasonable answer.

Ask it to recommend a company to your ideal customer, and your name might disappear.

That gap deserves attention.

A description of your services only tells part of the story. The more valuable question is whether AI connects those services with the people, problems, and situations you are best equipped to serve.

My view: **being understood by the right audience is what makes visibility valuable.**

## What the Research Found

In [research published by Search Engine Land](https://searchengineland.com/ai-knows-what-your-brand-does-but-not-who-its-for-500556), Emma Baker examined roughly 2,000 scored brand mentions involving five dental software brands across four AI search platforms.

The systems generally understood the product category. They were less reliable at identifying the right customer, preserving meaningful differences, and presenting accurate supporting evidence.

The intended customer was correctly identified in only 60% of scored responses. Some answers also introduced unsupported prices, capabilities, or other details.

The study covered one industry, so its percentages should not be treated as universal benchmarks. It also did not test whether particular content changes fixed these problems.

Still, the question it raises applies to any business:

**When someone describes a problem you solve, does AI recognize that your brand belongs in the conversation?**

## What It Means to “Teach” AI Who You Serve

For a business owner, teaching AI is best understood as improving the public information it can discover, interpret, and use.

Publishing a page does not directly retrain every model. Different systems use different sources, and no content formula guarantees a recommendation.

What you can do is make your audience, capabilities, and evidence easier to find and harder to misunderstand.

[Google’s guidance for AI search features](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features) reinforces the fundamentals: useful content, accessible pages, internal links, and important information available as text. Google does not require special AI markup or a new machine-readable file for inclusion.

The steps below are how I would apply those principles to brand positioning. They are practical recommendations to test, rather than a proven formula for controlling AI answers.

## 1. Describe Your Customer as Clearly as Your Service

Many websites devote paragraphs to what they offer and barely a sentence to who benefits most.

“Helping businesses grow” leaves a lot unanswered.

What kind of business? At what stage? Facing which problem? With what resources or constraints?

Start with a positioning statement that connects four things:

**Who you serve + the problem they face + what you provide + the outcome you help them pursue.**

For example, a hypothetical agency might say:

> We help established home service companies that need consistent lead generation but do not have an in-house marketing team, combining website strategy, local search, and campaign management.

That sentence gives a reader something concrete to evaluate.

Use accurate audience language in the homepage introduction, relevant service pages, and company description. Describe the customer’s situation in recognizable terms.

## 2. Give Distinct Audiences Their Own Useful Pages

A business can serve several audiences successfully. Its website should explain how the service fits each one.

Consider an answering service that works with medical practices, home service contractors, and law firms. Each audience has different questions about call handling, escalation, documentation, and availability.

A useful audience page should explain:

- The type of organization and decision-maker it serves.

- The situations that create a need for the service.

- The workflows or capabilities relevant to that audience.

- Requirements, limitations, and service boundaries.

- Evidence from comparable customers.

- The appropriate next step.

My recommendation is to start with your highest-priority audiences and build substantive pages for them.

Avoid producing dozens of nearly identical pages with an industry name swapped into the heading. Each page should help that particular buyer make a decision.

## 3. Answer the Questions People Ask While Choosing

Your ideal customer may never ask, “Who provides integrated digital solutions?”

They may ask:

> Which marketing agency can help a plumbing company generate local leads without hiring a marketing manager?

Or:

> What answering service can handle after-hours calls for a contractor and escalate urgent requests?

These questions contain the buying criteria: business type, problem, operating situation, and desired capability.

Build your content around those criteria.

Look through sales conversations, discovery notes, customer emails, and lost opportunities. Identify the questions that repeatedly determine whether someone is a good fit.

Then answer them directly on the relevant pages.

Useful topics include service coverage, integrations, onboarding, pricing factors, minimum commitments, and what happens when a customer’s needs change.

[Bing’s guidance on search intent](https://blogs.bing.com/webmaster/2024/11/The-Value-of-Intent/) emphasizes understanding what audiences are seeking and evaluating results through traffic and conversions. My application is to carry that intent into the details buyers use to choose a provider.

## 4. Put Evidence Beside the Audience Claim

“Experienced in healthcare” is a claim.

A case study describing the type of healthcare organization, its communication problem, the work completed, and the measured outcome gives that claim substance.

Connect the evidence directly to the audience you want to reach.

For each priority segment, publish what you can substantiate:

- A relevant case study.

- A customer testimonial used with permission.

- Applicable credentials or verified capabilities.

- Results with a timeframe and a clear explanation of what was measured.

Keep the context attached to the number. A result achieved for one customer should not read like a promise to every customer.

[Bing’s publisher guidance](https://blogs.bing.com/webmaster/2026/2/Introducing-AI-Performance-in-Bing-Webmaster-Tools-Public-Preview/) recommends supporting claims with evidence and improving content clarity and completeness. That supports the approach, although it does not establish that adding a case study will earn a recommendation.

## 5. Explain the Boundaries of Your Offer

Good positioning helps people recognize when a service fits their needs.

It should also explain the conditions.

If pricing is customized, state what determines it. If you serve particular locations, name them. If a capability requires an integration or a specific plan, make that condition visible.

For example:

> Pricing is customized according to monthly call volume, coverage hours, and escalation requirements.

That gives a buyer useful information without requiring a fixed public price.

Distinguish current features from planned features. Explain whether a service belongs to the parent company, a subsidiary, or a specific product.

My recommendation is to review the details prospects most often misunderstand and make those answers easy to locate. Clear boundaries reduce ambiguity, even though they cannot eliminate AI errors.

## 6. Align the Places That Describe Your Business

Your website may describe a specialist. An old directory listing may describe a generalist. A partner profile may still list a service you stopped providing.

Audit the public descriptions that are most relevant to your customers:

- Business and professional profiles.

- Industry directories and association listings.

- Partner pages.

- Review platforms.

- Published interviews and company announcements.

Correct outdated facts where you have access, and request corrections elsewhere.

Keep the underlying facts consistent while writing naturally for each platform. The goal is a coherent account of who you serve and what you do.

Third-party coverage is most useful when it contains accurate, specific information. Seek opportunities to share relevant expertise and documented work rather than simply accumulating mentions.

## 7. Make Your Positioning Accessible

A strong audience statement has limited value if it appears only inside an image or in a document behind a login.

Put essential positioning and supporting evidence in readable page text. Link audience pages from relevant service pages and connect case studies to the services they demonstrate.

Have your technical team check that priority pages can be crawled and indexed, and that security settings are not unintentionally blocking the search services you want to reach.

For Google’s AI search features, pages must be indexed and eligible to appear with a snippet. Any structured data should agree with the visible content. Meeting those requirements makes a page eligible; it does not guarantee inclusion.

## 8. Test Whether the Right Buyers Are Being Matched

Asking AI to describe your brand is a useful accuracy check. It does not test whether an unfamiliar buyer will discover you.

Create a small set of realistic, unbranded questions based on your priority customers.

Include different situations: a single-location business, a growing company, a buyer with an integration requirement, or someone needing support in a particular region.

Run those questions across the platforms your customers use. Record the date, platform, exact prompt, answer, and cited sources.

Evaluate several things separately:

| What to check | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Does your brand appear? | Whether you were included in this answer |
| Is your audience described correctly? | Whether the system understands customer fit |
| Are capabilities and limitations accurate? | Whether the recommendation could mislead a buyer |
| Is relevant evidence included? | Whether the answer gives a credible reason to consider you |
| Which sources are cited? | Where to investigate missing or outdated information |

Repeat the checks over time. Treat the results as a sample, not a complete picture of every customer’s experience.

Then connect that monitoring to business outcomes: qualified inquiries, sales conversations, and customers who actually fit your offer.

## Start With One Audience That Matters

You do not need to rewrite your entire website at once.

Choose one valuable customer segment. Clarify the positioning, improve its page, add relevant evidence, and correct conflicting descriptions. Record the changes and monitor what happens.

The research identifies a gap between recognizing a brand and understanding its customer. Closing that gap requires clearer information and ongoing testing.

That is the work I would prioritize.

**Give people, and the systems helping them choose, a clear reason to recognize that your brand is for them.**
