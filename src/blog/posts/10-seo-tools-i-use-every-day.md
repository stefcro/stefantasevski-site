I've been doing SEO for more than ten years, for eCommerce stores, B2B companies and agencies. I've tried a lot of tools in that time. Most of them I cancelled after a month.

These are the 10 I still use every single day. Some are free, some are expensive, and one of them I'm still experimenting with. None of them paid me to be here, and I'll tell you what bugs me about each one too.

They're roughly in order of how much I'd miss them.

## My daily SEO stack at a glance

| Tool | What I use it for | Free or paid |
|---|---|---|
| Google Search Console | Real clicks, queries and indexing | Free |
| Ahrefs | Backlinks and competitor research | Paid |
| Semrush | Keyword research and keyword gaps | Paid |
| SE Ranking | Rank tracking and client reports | Paid |
| Microsoft Clarity | Dead clicks, user paths, internal links | Free |
| Screaming Frog | Technical crawls and audits | Free up to 500 URLs |
| AnswerThePublic | Long-tail questions | Limited free searches |
| AlsoAsked | People Also Ask trees and H2s | Limited free searches |
| Claude | Analysis, regex, briefs, proofreading | Free and paid plans |
| KeySearch | Budget keyword research | Paid |

## 1. Google Search Console

<figure class="tool-shot"><img src="/assets/blog/seo-tools/google-search-console-performance.webp" alt="Google Search Console Performance report with 12 months of clicks and impressions for an eCommerce site" width="1600" height="876" loading="lazy"><figcaption>The Performance report for an eCommerce client over 12 months (property and queries blurred).</figcaption></figure>

**Best for:** first-party search data and indexing · **Price:** free

If you only use one tool from this list, use this one. Every other SEO tool is guessing. Search Console is Google telling you what actually happened.

I use it every morning. I check clicks and impressions for the pages I care about, look at which queries are growing, and see which pages dropped. When I start with a new client, this is the first access I ask for, before any paid tool.

What I use it for the most:

- **Performance report** filtered by page, to see every query one URL ranks for. This is where I find the pages that sit in positions 5 to 15 and only need a push.
- **Regex filters** to split branded and non-branded queries, or to pull every question query ("how", "what", "why").
- **URL Inspection** to check how Google sees a page and to request indexing after I publish or fix something.
- **Pages report** to see what's indexed, what's "discovered" and what's excluded, and why.

What I like: it's free, it's accurate, and it's Google's own data.

What bugs me: the interface only shows 1,000 rows, a lot of queries are hidden for privacy, and data is a few days behind. And this November it showed a spike of bot clicks that made a lot of sites look better than they were. I wrote about how I checked that in [Understand the Click Before You Chase It](/blog/understand-the-click-before-you-chase-it/).

## 2. Ahrefs

<figure class="tool-shot"><img src="/assets/blog/seo-tools/ahrefs-keywords-explorer.webp" alt="Ahrefs Keywords Explorer showing search volume trends for a German keyword list" width="1600" height="721" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Ahrefs Keywords Explorer, from a German eCommerce project I work on.</figcaption></figure>

**Best for:** backlinks and competitor research · **Price:** paid (free Webmaster Tools for your own sites)

Ahrefs is the tool I open when I need to understand a competitor. Who links to them, which pages bring them traffic, which keywords they rank for that we don't.

I use Site Explorer almost daily. When a client asks "why does this competitor outrank us?", the answer usually starts in their backlink profile and their top pages report. For link building work I live in the referring domains and the "best by links" reports. That's how I find the pages that earn links on their own, and the sites that might link to us too.

I've also started experimenting with their AI visibility features to see how often a brand shows up in AI answers. It's early, but I think this will matter more every year. I wrote about the bigger picture in [AI Knows Your Brand. Does It Know Who It's For?](/blog/ai-knows-your-brand-does-it-know-who-its-for/)

What I like: the backlink index is the one I trust most, and the data is easy to read even for clients.

What bugs me: it's expensive, and the traffic estimates can be far off when I compare them with a client's real Search Console data. I use it for direction and comparison, never as the truth.

## 3. Semrush

<figure class="tool-shot"><img src="/assets/blog/seo-tools/semrush-keyword-magic-tool.webp" alt="Semrush Keyword Magic Tool with keyword groups, intent, volume and keyword difficulty for the seed keyword online shop" width="1600" height="995" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Semrush’s Keyword Magic Tool. Image: Semrush Knowledge Base.</figcaption></figure>

**Best for:** keyword research and keyword gaps · **Price:** paid

Semrush and Ahrefs overlap a lot. People ask me why I pay for both. My honest answer: they're good at different things, and I like having a second opinion.

I use Semrush mostly for keyword research. The Keyword Magic Tool is my favorite way to build a big list around one topic and then filter it by intent. I also use the Keyword Gap report a lot. You put in your domain and three or four competitors, and it shows the keywords they rank for and you don't. For a content strategy, that's often the first list I work from.

What I like: huge keyword database, good intent labels, and the gap report saves me hours.

What bugs me: the interface tries to do everything, so it can feel crowded. I've used it for years and I still click around looking for things sometimes.

## 4. SE Ranking

<figure class="tool-shot"><img src="/assets/blog/seo-tools/se-ranking-rank-tracking.webp" alt="SE Ranking detailed rankings report with top 1, top 3 and top 10 position filters for Google Germany" width="1600" height="826" loading="lazy"><figcaption>SE Ranking’s detailed rankings view. I watch the TOP 3 and TOP 10 counts every day.</figcaption></figure>

**Best for:** rank tracking and client reporting · **Price:** paid, cheaper than the two above

I use SE Ranking for one main job: tracking rankings every day. I've used rank trackers inside Ahrefs and Semrush too, but for daily tracking of a set keyword list I prefer SE Ranking. It's affordable, it's fast, and the reports are clean enough to send to clients without much editing.

I set up projects per market and per device, tag keywords by page type (category, product, blog), and watch the top 3 and top 10 counts. The top 3 is where the clicks are, so that's the number I track hardest.

What I like: price for value, accurate daily tracking, simple client reports.

What bugs me: the backlink and keyword research data is thinner than Ahrefs or Semrush. I don't use it for that.

## 5. Microsoft Clarity

<figure class="tool-shot"><img src="/assets/blog/seo-tools/microsoft-clarity-session-recording.webp" alt="Microsoft Clarity session recording of stefantasevski.com with AI session insights flagging a dead click" width="1600" height="857" loading="lazy"><figcaption>A Clarity recording from this site. Its AI session insights flagged a slow first load, which I then fixed.</figcaption></figure>

**Best for:** dead clicks, user paths and internal links · **Price:** free

This one surprises people. Clarity isn't an "SEO tool", but I use it every day, and I have it running on this site too.

Rankings get people to a page. Clarity shows me what they do once they're there. I use it for three things:

- **Dead clicks.** People click on things that aren't links: a product image, a bolded phrase, a card title. Every dead click is someone asking for a link that doesn't exist. I add those links.
- **How people move between pages.** I watch recordings of visitors who land from organic search and see where they go next, or where they give up.
- **Internal linking.** Heatmaps show me which internal links get used and which ones nobody sees because they sit too low on the page. This is where a lot of my internal linking ideas come from. I went deeper on this in [Your Next SEO Win Could Be an Internal Link](/blog/your-next-seo-win-could-be-an-internal-link/).

What I like: it's completely free, it takes five minutes to install, and it connects to GA4.

What bugs me: watching recordings can eat a whole afternoon if you're not careful. I always go in with one question and filter the sessions first.

## 6. Screaming Frog

<figure class="tool-shot"><img src="/assets/blog/seo-tools/screaming-frog-seo-spider.webp" alt="Screaming Frog SEO Spider crawl showing response code issues, canonical issues and issue details" width="1600" height="914" loading="lazy"><figcaption>A Screaming Frog crawl of a client store (URLs blurred). The Issues tab is where every audit starts for me.</figcaption></figure>

**Best for:** technical SEO audits · **Price:** free up to 500 URLs, paid licence after that

Every technical audit I do starts with a Screaming Frog crawl. I crawl the site the way Google would, then look for broken links, redirect chains, missing or duplicate titles, canonicals pointing to the wrong place, and pages blocked by robots or noindex.

The part I use most is internal link data. I check how many internal links each important page gets, and I look for orphan pages by connecting the crawl to Search Console and GA4. I also use custom extraction to pull things like prices, schema or author names from every page, which is great for eCommerce sites.

What I like: it does exactly what I tell it, it's very fast, and it's cheap for what it does.

What bugs me: it looks like software from 2010, and on very big sites you need a strong machine or database storage mode. It's not beginner-friendly either. I'd give it to a junior only after showing them around.

## 7. AnswerThePublic

<figure class="tool-shot"><img src="/assets/blog/seo-tools/answerthepublic-question-wheel.webp" alt="AnswerThePublic question wheel grouping questions about marketing by search engines, AI models, social media and shopping" width="1100" height="1100" loading="lazy"><figcaption>AnswerThePublic’s question wheel. Image: AnswerThePublic.</figcaption></figure>

**Best for:** finding long-tail questions · **Price:** a few free searches per day, paid plans for more

When I plan content for a topic, I want to know how real people phrase their questions. AnswerThePublic takes one keyword and shows the questions around it, grouped by who, what, why, how and where, plus comparisons like "X vs Y".

I use it at the start of content planning, before I open the paid tools. It gives me the language of the audience. Many of these long-tail questions have little or no volume in the paid tools, but people still search them, and they're exactly what people ask AI assistants too.

What I like: fast, visual, and it shows phrasing I wouldn't think of.

What bugs me: there's no real volume data on the free plan, and the free searches run out quickly.

## 8. AlsoAsked

<figure class="tool-shot"><img src="/assets/blog/seo-tools/alsoasked-people-also-ask.webp" alt="AlsoAsked People Also Ask question tree for the query search engine optimization" width="1600" height="851" loading="lazy"><figcaption>AlsoAsked’s question tree for “search engine optimization”.</figcaption></figure>

**Best for:** People Also Ask trees and H2 ideas · **Price:** a few free searches, paid plans for more

AlsoAsked shows the People Also Ask questions Google displays for a query, and the questions that open up under each one. It's a map of what people want to know next.

I use it mainly to structure articles. The first level of questions often becomes my H2s, and the next level becomes the FAQ or sub-sections. It also helps me decide what a page shouldn't cover, because a branch that goes in a different direction usually deserves its own page.

What I like: the questions come straight from Google, and the tree view makes the structure obvious.

What bugs me: it's narrow. It does one thing, and you'll run out of free searches fast if you're planning a lot of content.

## 9. Claude

**Best for:** analysis, regex, briefs and proofreading · **Price:** free plan, paid plans for heavier use

Claude is the one I experiment with the most. I'm still finding new ways to use it, and some of them stick.

Right now I use it to:

- Write regex for Search Console filters, which I used to do by trial and error.
- Make sense of big exports. I paste a keyword or page export and ask it to group keywords by intent or find patterns I missed.
- Draft content briefs and outlines, which I then rewrite with what I actually know about the client.
- Check schema markup and proofread copy before it goes live.

What I like: it's very good with long documents and data, and it's honest when it doesn't know something.

What bugs me: you still have to check everything. It will sound confident about things it got wrong, so I never publish or send anything from it without reading it myself. I use it as an assistant, and I'm still the one making the calls.

## 10. KeySearch

<figure class="tool-shot"><img src="/assets/blog/seo-tools/keysearch-serp-analysis.webp" alt="KeySearch SERP analysis table with page authority, domain authority and links for each ranking page" width="866" height="551" loading="lazy"><figcaption>KeySearch’s SERP analysis, where I check how strong page one is. Image: KeySearch.</figcaption></figure>

**Best for:** budget keyword research · **Price:** paid, one of the cheapest options

KeySearch is my quick keyword check. When I want a fast read on volume and difficulty without opening a big suite, or when I'm working on a smaller site or a side project, I use KeySearch.

I like its difficulty score for finding easier keywords, and the competitor view shows me how strong the pages on page one are. For smaller budgets it covers most of what you need.

What I like: low price, simple interface, good enough data for small and mid-size sites.

What bugs me: the database is smaller than Ahrefs or Semrush, so for big eCommerce or competitive B2B markets I don't rely on it alone.

## How I'd choose if I were starting today

You don't need all ten. If I had to start again with no budget, I'd use Search Console, Clarity, the free version of Screaming Frog and the free searches in AnswerThePublic and AlsoAsked. That covers real data, user behavior, technical health and content ideas.

When there's budget, I'd add one big suite first (Ahrefs or Semrush, whichever you find easier to use), and then a dedicated rank tracker like SE Ranking.

And whatever you use, the tools only show you where to look. Deciding what matters for the business is still your job.
